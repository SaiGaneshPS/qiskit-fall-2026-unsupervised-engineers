3 · How do we know it passes? Classical vs quantum

Nobody can observe next winter, so blackout hours have to be estimated by simulation. The hard part: blackouts are rare, so you need many runs before you can say on which side of the 2.8-hour line a plan sits. Both methods estimate the same number for this plan.

Classical way: Monte Carlo

Simulate random winter hours one at a time (weather, demand, which plants break) and count how many are short of power.

runs to be sure if it passes

Quantum way: amplitude estimation

Put every possible hour into one quantum state at once, then repeat a “boost” that makes the short-of-power hours more likely to show up. Measuring how fast they grow tells you how rare they are.

4 · Where normal computers struggle: a 3-day cold snap

In a real cold snap, a broken plant can stay broken for days. Then there is no exact shortcut: every plant’s history has to be followed day by day, and the possibilities double with every plant and every day. Planners fall back on Monte Carlo. Here the quantum method checks four plans through the winter’s worst 3-day cold snap, using the real 22-qubit circuits, with the same number of runs as Monte Carlo. A plan is safe if the chance of a day short of power stays under 10% (about one day in ten years).
